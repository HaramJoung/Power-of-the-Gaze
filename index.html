00 · BRIEF AND SITE

Overview

Every person has an unconscious desire to observe others. That desire becomes ownership of the gaze, and ownership becomes power and control.

The brief was to invent a creative, systematic way of investigating one small, specific site, and to see what a designer’s way of looking could reveal there. I chose the courtyard outside my window.

Three weeks of watching it turned into a question about who owns the gaze in shared space, and later into furniture, a participatory workshop tool, a campaign and the game above.

The vantage point: a third-floor room · with the curtain half drawn I could watch without being seen

01 · WEEK 1

Point of Views

From a room on the third floor I could see students eating, talking and moving chairs, the weather changing and the guards walking past. I tried four methods, and only later saw that each was a different point of view.

02 · WEEK 2

Tracking Invisible Things

I tried to record what the eye cannot see: heat left on benches, light, bodies moving after dark. Through the window bars, every frame started to resemble surveillance footage.

03 · WEEK 3

Gaze

Asking myself why I wanted to capture invisible things, I found a voyeuristic interest in other people’s lives. The thermal camera had not been a tool for discovery; I had been drawn to watching.

1 / 6

Why am I trying to capture invisible things? · documenting the unnoticed · a voyeuristic interest · outside the courtyard (my room) and inside it

Curtains kept the watchers hidden from outside, while the people in the yard were fully exposed.

The panopticon puts a dark tower among lit cells so only the guard can see. The courtyard works the same way: students below cannot tell whether anyone is behind the windows.

The same week I imagined a campaign for people who are stared at every day in public, such as people living on the street, whose stories are rarely heard.

04 · WRITTEN RESPONSE

Behind the curtain

Through a photographic investigation of the voyeuristic stance in the yard of my residence hall, I looked at the double vision architecture creates and the power structures that follow from it.

Georges Perec: “Force yourself to write down what is of no interest, what is most obvious, most common, most colourless.” Sustained, almost obsessive photographing produced a sense of otherness even in a dormitory. Each photograph worked as evidence that removed a blind spot.

Patrick Keiller’s The View from the Train showed me the double status of architecture: a house’s inside cannot readily be seen from outside. It let me tie the space, the students and my own actions into a small history of the panopticon.

05 · OUTCOME

Poly-Opticon

Panopticon → Polyopticon

Modern public space is all-encompassing surveillance; the gaze cannot be removed. So Poly-Opticon multiplies it, with furniture that lets people hide one side of the body.

PIECEWHAT IT DOES
Communal tablesDrawn from the courtyard’s own lines
Shielded seatOne side shielded, for private moments
Facing pairHide completely or reveal parts of yourself; a spectator by choice
Reclining wedgeFor lying back on the grass in the sun

The colours came from a video call that froze into a blur on a bad connection.

So what

Implications for shared space

01

Visibility is uneven

In the courtyard, windows above can watch while people below are exposed and cannot tell who is looking.

02

Design can redistribute privacy

Poly-Opticon does not remove the gaze. Its furniture lets people choose how much of themselves to show.

03

Let residents negotiate exposure

A participatory workshop tool let peers discuss how exposed each spot felt before anything was built.
